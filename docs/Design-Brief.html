The product

What it is, and why it needs to exist

A mobile app that gives men undergoing Ayurvedic treatment for infertility a clear, do-able set of supportive food preparations — Vajikarana Āhāra — each grounded in both classical Ayurvedic texts and modern nutrition guidance.

Today this knowledge is scattered across Sanskrit classics and verbal clinical advice — there's no single, organized, practical digital resource. The app makes it findable, understandable, and followable. In UX terms it is a curated recipe library for a sensitive health context.

The design tension to hold

Not a lifestyle food app. Not a clinical dashboard. It lives in between — warm heritage credibility + modern app clarity. Every screen should feel like it belongs to a trusted practitioner, never a diet-fad startup.

The audience

Who you're designing for

Design for the patient first. The clinician is a lens on the same content, not a second app.

Primary · the patient

A man managing a stigmatized, stressful diagnosis

Roughly 25–45+, mixed digital literacy, often cooking in a kitchen. He wants discretion, reassurance, and “what do I make tonight” — not a lecture. He is privacy-conscious about health data and may not want an account.

Secondary · the clinician

Ayurvedic physician, PG scholar, or dietitian

Counsels patients and may recommend recipes. Needs to trust the sources (they'll check the references), point patients to specific dishes, and search fast by ingredient, benefit, or classical name.

  • Discretion by default — a neutral name & icon; nothing on the lock screen, home icon, or notifications that “outs” him at a glance.
  • Kitchen-ready — big text, offline access to saved recipes, keep-screen-awake, forgiving greasy-finger tap targets.
  • Calm & hopeful tone — encouraging, plain-language, never clinical-cold or shaming.

North stars

Seven design principles

Discretion & dignity.The sensitive topic makes privacy the #1 differentiator. Private-feeling, neutral, never embarrassing.
Credible, evidence-anchored.Every recipe cites classical texts and modern nutrition. Make that visible — it's the trust engine.
Calm & warm, not clinical.Grounded and reassuring. Avoid both hospital-sterile and new-age gimmick.
Do-able above all.He must be able to shop for and cook it. Clarity of ingredients, quantity and steps beats decoration.
Modern heritage.Honor Ayurvedic & Sanskrit roots with restraint and taste — not clip-art mandalas.
Safety-first.Contraindications (diabetes, obesity, lactose…) must never be buried.
Accessible & inclusive.Bilingual terms, readable type, works for older / low-literacy users, works offline.

1
Name (dual)

Friendly English name + the classical Sanskrit name. English = approachable, Sanskrit = heritage credibility. Decide which leads. Must render diacritics (ā, ṣ, ṛ).

2
Sources

1–3 classical texts + a modern guideline. The trust signal — clinicians will check. Present but expandable, not clutter.

3
Image

⚠ Biggest visual risk. Sourcing is mixed — real photos, AI-generated, and dead links. You need one plan: consistent photography, a unified illustration set (shown here), or a branded placeholder.

4
Ingredients (dual units)

Always metric + household measure, often as ranges (“80–100 g”). Consider a units toggle; keep it scannable for shopping.

5
Method (phased)

Sequential steps — sometimes grouped into phases (chena → dough → fry → stuff → syrup) and sometimes offering alternatives (“oven / air-fryer / pan”). Design a Cook Mode that handles both.

6
Yield & time

“Total quantity” + shelf-life. Many recipes need overnight soaks or multi-day prep — surface a “start ahead” flag.

7
Contraindications

On sweets & dairy. Must be unmissable — warning treatment, not fine print. Tie to the user's profile if personalization exists.

Information architecture

Organize the way a patient thinks

The source files are filed by texture — solid, liquid, semi-solid. That's the author's filing system, not how a hungry patient searches (“something for breakfast,” “a dish with amla,” “a safe sweet”).

Keep texture as one filter, but lead discovery with patient-friendly facets. 83 items is too many for a flat list, but rich enough for real filtering.

Watch-outs

UX considerations & edge cases

These are specific to this content — they'll bite in hi-fi if ignored now.

  • Contraindication safety. If onboarding captures conditions, warn or filter unsafe recipes. Even without a profile, keep the warning visually strong.
  • Image inconsistency. Photos, AI images, and dead links are mixed together. Decide photography vs. one illustration system vs. branded placeholder — before hi-fi.
  • Sanskrit & diacritics. Names carry ā, ṣ, ṛ. Pick fonts that render them; add pronunciation to the glossary; make search diacritic-insensitive.
  • Dual measurements & ranges. Always metric + household, or a toggle; handle “80–100 g” gracefully.
  • Prep-ahead timings. Overnight soaks, multi-day preserves — surface “start the night before” up front.
  • Alternative & optional steps. Content offers “OR bake / OR air-fry” and “(optional)” items — design for variants, not one rigid list.
  • Offline & kitchen ergonomics. Saved recipes + Cook Mode must work with no signal; screen-awake; large hit areas.
  • Privacy. Minimize data, no ad-tracking, usable anonymously; be explicit about what's stored.
  • Empty / loading / error states. No results, no saved items, failed image — design them; they're frequent here.

Visual direction · a starting point, not a mandate

“Modern Ayurveda”

Calm · grounded · trustworthy · warm · natural. Heritage credibility with contemporary restraint — the opposite of both a spice-box cliché and a sterile clinic. This very page is one interpretation; react to it, then make it yours.
